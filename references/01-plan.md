# Step 1: Plan

Decide these things before any layout or colour. Write the answers down in a few lines; you will need them at the checkpoint.

## Four questions

1. **Who is this page for?** One kind of visitor, in one sentence. "A homeowner in the area with water on the floor" is useful. "Customers" is not.
2. **What is the one action?** Call, request a quote, book, buy, sign up. Pick one. Everything on the page either leads to it or earns the trust needed for it.
3. **What would make this visitor trust the business?** Usually: what they do, where they work, how fast they answer, proof they are real. Note which of these the brief actually supplies.
4. **What is the visitor worried about?** Price surprises, no-shows, being oversold, poor work. The page should answer the worry with facts from the brief, not with adjectives.

## Structure

Start from the visitor's questions in the order they ask them, not from a template.

A sound default order for a local service business:

1. Hero: what the business does, where, and the primary action.
2. Services: what they can be called for.
3. Why this business: the real differences supplied in the brief.
4. How it works: what happens after the visitor calls. Use only when the process is not obvious.
5. Service area and hours.
6. Proof: reviews, accreditations, past work. Only if supplied. Otherwise leave it out, and list it in the handover.
7. Contact: the primary action again, with every way to reach the business.

Change the order when the business calls for it. An emergency trade leads with the phone number and response. A product leads with the product. A person leads with their work.

Cut any section you cannot fill with real content. Five strong sections beat nine thin ones.

## Style

Choose a style that fits the trade and the visitor, and name it in one sentence a client would understand. Derive it from this business: its materials, its setting, its customers, how it wants to be seen. Two businesses in the same trade should not end up with the same sentence. Do not reuse a phrase from this file; the examples here show the form, not the answer.

The form: "[character in two or three plain words], like [something concrete from the business's world]: [dominant colour], [how much space], [the accent and what it is for]."

Take the style from the business, not from what is fashionable:

- Trades and services: sturdy, clear, high contrast, large type, obvious buttons.
- Professional services: calm, spacious, restrained colour.
- Food, hospitality, beauty: photo-led, warm, more character in the type.
- Software and products: precise, product shown early, tighter spacing.

## Palette

- One dominant colour, one accent, and a set of neutrals. That is the whole palette.
- The dominant colour carries the brand feeling and covers large areas or the main text colour. The accent is for the primary action and almost nothing else. If the accent appears everywhere it stops meaning "act here".
- Pick hues that belong to the trade or its materials: water, copper, timber, stone, foliage, safety colours. Avoid defaulting to purple, violet or a blue-to-purple gradient; those read as generated.
- Tint the neutrals slightly towards the dominant hue. Pure grey on pure white looks unconsidered.
- Give every colour a role and a hex value: background, surface, text, muted text, border, dominant, accent, accent text.
- Check contrast now, not later: body text at least 4.5:1 against its background, large text and interface elements at least 3:1. Text on the accent colour must pass too.

## Type

- Two typefaces at most: one with character for headings, one plain and readable for text. One family for both is fine when it has enough weights.
- Choose the heading face for the business's character: a sturdy grotesque for a trade, a serif for a practice or a restaurant, a geometric sans for a product.
- Avoid the faces every generated page uses by default unless the brand supplies them. Look through the catalogue for a face that suits this business instead of reaching for the first one that comes to mind; if you have used a face on a previous page, that is a reason to look again, not a reason to reuse it.
- Limit weights to three: regular, medium or semibold, and bold.

## When a brand is supplied

A supplied logo, colour, typeface or existing site is a fixed input.

- Use the supplied colours as the dominant and accent. Build the neutrals around them. Do not add a second accent of your own.
- Use the supplied typeface. Add a second face only if the supplied one cannot do body text.
- Match the tone of existing copy.
- If a supplied colour fails contrast for text, keep it for surfaces and large elements and use a darker or lighter shade of the same hue for text. Say so in the direction summary.
